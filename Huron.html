Decision quality, not tool count: Making AI's value visible from the floor to the boardroom

The question consuming healthcare boardrooms is no longer whether to invest in AI. That debate is over, and the capital is already moving. The harder question is one of returns: even as 81% of health system C-suite leaders plan to grow their AI investment in fiscal year 2026, according to Huron's 2026 Executive Provider research,1 few can articulate what their previous investments actually delivered.

The stakes of leaving that question unanswered keep rising. Nearly half of executives are actively preparing for federal reimbursement cuts, margins remain compressed, and every technology dollar competes with clinical and workforce priorities. Yet AI ranked as the industry's top strategic concern, top leadership challenge, and top investment priority in the research, which surveyed 300 executives across national, regional, and academic health systems. The real issue is why the value so often fails to materialize where the leaders funding it can see it.

The research offers a clear diagnosis: senior leaders and the VPs and directors they work with disagree about whether their AI initiatives are paying off. Nearly a quarter of C-suite leaders say none of their AI initiatives have generated measurable ROI. Ask the VPs and directors running those same initiatives and the picture flips: not one reports zero return. From where they sit, the wins are visible: hours recovered in a clinic, claims moving with fewer touches. But most of those wins never get a baseline, so they never roll up to the ledger the C-suite reads.

When AI frees up clinician hours or takes touches out of a claim, the time savings does not convert itself. Someone has to convert that gain into something measurable, such as more visits, lower staffing spend, or reduced clinician turnover. In our work with health systems, we often see this opportunity stall because responsibility is never formally assigned to the right owners: the leaders who have the authority to redesign workflows and reallocate resources, and the willingness to make the hard calls that conversion demands, such as consolidating shifts or reshaping roles. Too often that mandate falls through the cracks because ownership is unclear and the authority to act is fragmented across multiple stakeholders. Freed capacity quietly reverts to slightly lighter days rather than measurable margin impact.

An unassigned responsibility to the right owners is a solvable problem, which makes this diagnosis more encouraging than it sounds. Some AI solutions underdeliver, and some deployments should rightfully be shut down. But technology failure is rarely the whole story. More often, the missing ROI traces to a value target that was never given an owner, a baseline, or a destination. ROI is simply value that has been measured all the way to the ledger. Closing that distance is a matter of leadership, not technology. The health systems that pull ahead will be the ones that treat it that way, with intentional decisions around which AI solutions to scale and which to decommission and holding the right individuals accountable for the value delivery.

Capacity is not value until someone converts it

In practice, converted value takes one of three forms in a health system, and each has a natural owner on the executive team. Recovered capacity can become revenue growth: added visits, added procedures, and shorter backlogs. It can become lower premium-labor spend, with agency, overtime, and locums retired. Or it can lead to reduced clinician and employee turnover.

Naming the owner before go-live gives the recovered hours a purpose from day one, with someone accountable for turning them into a specific result. They become added visits, retired agency shifts, a clinician who stays, or fewer after-hours charting sessions. When no one is named, the hours get absorbed into business as usual and never show up on a financial statement.

One COO interviewed in the research described how that outcome looks from the budget: "Here's the dilemma. How do we pay for it? We're doing the same amount of work, and we just added a bunch of expenses." That is not a poorly run deployment. It is a successful one with no conversion designed. The technology delivered, and the organization still cannot find the return.

That view is common at the top. When leaders grade their full AI portfolios, 67% of C-suite leaders say fewer than a quarter of their initiatives are generating returns, while only 21% of VPs and directors see it that way. The people closest to the work also have an explanation. Asked what is holding results back, VPs and directors point less to the technology than to whether clinicians and staff actually adopt it.

The pattern has a precedent, and it is the one we share with boards. Edison lit lower Manhattan in 1882, and American factories electrified quickly. Yet measured productivity barely moved for roughly three decades, because factories swapped the steam engine for one large central electric motor and kept everything else: the same belts, line shafts, and multi-story layouts built around a single power source. The gains arrived in the 1920s with unit drive, a small motor at every machine, which let factories reorganize around the process rather than the power source. Most health systems are in that central motor phase today. Ambient documentation gets dropped into an unchanged clinic template. A coding engine gets layered onto charge capture that runs four different ways across four hospitals. The AI solution works. The factory has not changed yet.

Run AI like a portfolio, not a collection of pilots

The solution is less exotic than the problem. It looks mundane on paper, which is exactly why so few systems do it. It starts with one list detailing every AI project in the organization, including department-level pilots, vendor features quietly switched on inside the EHR, and undocumented solutions staff already use on their own. Most health systems cannot produce that list within a week. That inability is usually the first thing we discover when we begin working with a system.

Once the list exists, the real discipline begins. Every project on it carries three requirements: an executive who answers for the result in their own budget, a success measure recorded before the solution goes live, and a date when leaders will decide to expand it, rework it, or end it.

Some of AI's returns show up as dollars, and some do not. Both deserve the same rigor. The CFO owns the dollars: revenue cycle automation, prior authorization, patient throughput, and reduced agency and overtime spending. The CMO, CNO, and CHRO own the outcomes that resist a price tag: clinician wellbeing, patient safety and experience, and competitive standing. Retention shows how the two ledgers meet. The CMO and CHRO own whether clinicians stay, and every clinician who does appears on the CFO's ledger as a replacement cost avoided. Hard to monetize is not the same as impossible to measure. Each outcome still gets a starting measurement, a way to track it, and a regular review. Boards already treat the two as equals: in our research, competitive positioning, financial ROI, and patient safety carried nearly identical weight on board agendas.

Turning AI into measurable value in healthcare

 

AI pilots are growing across healthcare. The bigger opportunity is turning those individual efforts into measurable impact across the organization.

Sequence, not spending, separates the leaders

Adoption across the industry has split. Roughly 38% of health systems are still in the early stages, ranging from initial exploration to discrete pilots, while 35% have reached broad deployment or frontier-level innovation. Both groups are spending. The difference between them is not budget. It is sequence. The leading systems start with an end-to-end journey rather than a single solution: the path a patient's care and its paperwork travel from referral to discharge to paid claim, or the workflow a nurse moves through in a shift. They standardize the work and clean up the data before expanding anything. They design the conversion before go-live. And they stop initiatives that are not demonstrating value.

We apply five tests before a project earns the right to grow. The results should beat the organization's own numbers from before the solution arrived, and someone should already own what the freed-up time or money becomes. The process should work the same way at the next hospital as at the first. Clinicians should actually be using the solution, not just holding licenses. And each new site should cost less than the last. Projects that cannot pass get paused, reworked, or retired. Retirement is what makes the rest credible: attention is the scarcest resource in most health systems, and every unprofitable pilot left running quietly consumes more of it.

That discipline is also what finance leaders are waiting to see. One regional CFO interviewed for the research put it plainly: "CFOs are very skeptical of any ROI, because you usually take whatever you're given, divide it by two, and then divide it by two again." A track record of honest measurement, including the losses, is what wins the next round of funding. A portfolio that has never stopped anything is not a portfolio. It is a collection.

Health systems also do not have unlimited time to put this rigor in place. Payers are already using automation across the revenue cycle: revenue cycle leaders describe an AI arms race, and executives in our interviews reported audit volumes rising as much as tenfold. With reimbursement already under pressure, a hospital appealing automated denials manually is losing ground it cannot afford. Keeping pace requires standing oversight of many systems and AI solutions running at once.

Decision quality, not tool count

None of this requires new capital or a new platform. What it requires is governance: the resolve to review the AI portfolio quarterly, alongside the organization's other major investments, and to act on what the reviews show. The test of whether it is working is simple. Each quarterly review should end something. A portfolio that stops nothing for four straight quarters is not being governed. It is being funded on autopilot. That willingness to stop certain initiatives will do more for 2027 returns than any AI solution bought in 2026.

The margin pressure shaping this decade is not going away, and neither is the technology. The health systems that win with AI will not be the ones with the most tools. They will be the ones whose AI investments match where the organization has chosen to focus and whose leaders can say, for every solution they fund, who owns the result, what the value is, and when the next decision is due.
