Behind the Research: Carolyn Fu

Before entering academia, Assistant Professor Carolyn Fu spent seven years as an engineer in Singapore, where questions about how complex systems work together led her to study innovation strategy. Today, she looks to the arts for insights into how organizations can bring together different ideas and expertise to innovate. We asked Fu about her research, background in the arts, and what she likes to do in her (nonexistent) free time.

What is your area of research and how did you become interested in this field?

I study innovation strategy. I was an engineer for seven years in Singapore, and my last role was in national security and critical infrastructure—services like water, power, security, transport that keep society up and running. Our project was to coordinate them into a massive model to better understand how they interact—if there’s a disaster in one sector, how it affects others—like if a powerplant goes down and airports shut down as a result.

It was a massive project. I discovered that everyone had completely different ideas and definitions of national security and resiliency. It was like a giant puzzle, to understand the different types of objective functions across all the industries. We thought that because we were in a centralized office with oversight, we could understand how all the pieces fit together—but it turns out that centralization also came with critical blind spots. That tension between handling things in a centralized or decentralized fashion is a classic question that goes back to the start of economics and business. I soon realized I was much more interested in that general question. The PhDs I was working with said I had “strong PhD energy.”

You’ve written a lot about the arts—ballet and opera—what drew you to that work and how does it connect with innovation strategy?

I've always been an artist. I did ballet seriously until university, when I had to decide whether to be an engineer or a choreographer, so it’s always been a big part of my life. When I went to MIT to do my PhD, I realized that the centralized/decentralized issue was exactly how the arts run—where individual artists are always experimenting with new forms of creativity, but have to come together to stage a production everyone agrees on.

All my work is in settings where there's a lot of variation—how people who are spread across a landscape of possible solutions come together, and aggregate them into one that works. Innovation used to focus on how you discover one good idea, but now especially with AI everyone is experimenting and we’re spoiled for choice with new ideas. The burden then shifts to how we manage this overwhelming set of possibilities. But the arts have been dealing with this forever, so I strongly feel that they lead the way for how to solve this problem.

Can you give us an example?

A lot of my research has been with the Royal Opera House (today's Royal Ballet and Opera) in London. Back when they brought in Pavarotti for an opera, he knew exactly the kind of production he wanted—which singers, producers, composers, designers, etc.—which wasn’t necessarily what the opera house wanted, nor what they hired him for. On the one hand that sounds like typical diva behavior. But the fact is he had worked in so many production configurations that he knew, better than any single opera house, how the different pieces across the whole ecosystem worked together. And so even though Pavarotti was making demands entirely outside his core competence as a singer, he was actually bringing essential knowledge of how different pieces of the puzzle fit together to best complement him.

To take that broader, it might sound ridiculous to hire a software engineer to dictate marketing or design, but because of how interconnected work has become, they probably have a pretty good idea of how all those pieces should come together. Finding the right combinations of skills and resources that complement one another can matter much more than finding individually good qualities, and in my opinion, we don't think enough about how to find or test for the former.

It comes back to the questions I started with in critical infrastructure and national security: What’s the balance between centralized and decentralized decision making when you want lots of good ideas from throughout the system, but performance ultimately comes down to how well they work together?

What are you working on now?

The big project is on August Wilson's American Century Cycle. He wrote 10 incredible plays, all of which were Tony nominated and have won 14 Tonys, two Pulitzers, and three Oscars now that Denzel Washington has brought them to Netflix. Through his plays, Wilson also lifted up a generation of Black artists, including Angela Bassett, Courtney B. Vance, Denzel Washington, and Viola Davis.

These plays were very atypical, with themes and dramaturgy that were so different from Broadway at the time—how did they manage to gain mainstream acceptance? I went down a rabbit hole to trace how every play began, starting in workshops at the Eugene O’Neill Theater Center, then through smaller theaters in different cities until it came to Broadway. He had to restructure the contracts for nonprofit theaters to stage the play and essentially buy futures in the eventual Broadway show—creating the foundations of today’s off-Broadway model.

That slow progression to Broadway allowed them to gradually improve the play—a classic model from an innovation standpoint. But it also gave the audience time to understand and appreciate what he was doing, something we don’t think about enough in innovation. Critics could use the off-Broadway ramp up to learn the unique themes, musicality and language deriving from the Black experience. Which comes back to the question of how we get people with different ideas across the landscape to agree on what an innovation should look like.

How do you go about this work; what does it look like day-to-day?

A lot of my projects are archival, so they take a long time. There’s a lot of sitting in basements. But sometimes that’s great because at the Royal Opera House, the archives were next to their rehearsal room, so I heard the best opera every day. Thanks to HBS, I’m not all by myself anymore; I have a small army of incredible research associates. We've scanned 20,000 pages of scripts from all the different locations until they got to Broadway. I'm in the middle of a massive data cleaning exercise to match them all.

I do mostly what's called inductive work, sitting and reading 40 years of meeting minutes to figure out what I think was happening. I then map all those insights into computational simulations—putting in different variables to see how things change if, say, Pavarotti or the Opera House chose people a different way. Under what conditions do different decisions and ideas come together to improve performance? That simulation can play with variables to figure out what firms in different settings should do.

What do you like to do in your spare time?

I don't really have that, so it's a great question! I've just signed up for ballet classes again because basically once I started my PhD, I sat down on a couch and haven't gotten off of it since. I also play piano. But my work covers so many of my rabbit hole hobbies—I don’t know how I managed that! I spend all my time reading scripts of plays, which is delightful, and somehow that’s my work.
